# Training intensity distribution in elite endurance athletes

## Observed distribution

Studies of successful cross-country skiers, rowers, runners and cyclists show a consistent pattern: roughly 80% of training sessions are performed below the first ventilatory threshold, where blood lactate stays under about 2 mmol/L. The remaining sessions are performed at high intensity, well above the second threshold. Very little time is spent in the zone between the thresholds.

A three-zone model is used for this analysis: zone 1 below the first lactate turn point, zone 2 between the thresholds, and zone 3 above the second threshold. When counted by session, elite athletes land near 80/20; when counted by accumulated minutes, the share of low-intensity time is even higher, often over 90%.

## Why the pattern works

Long low-intensity sessions build mitochondrial density, capillarisation and fat oxidation with little autonomic stress, so they can be repeated day after day. High-intensity sessions give a strong stimulus to maximal oxygen uptake but cost a lot of recovery. About two hard sessions per week seems to be the sustainable ceiling for most athletes. Moving more training into the threshold zone adds fatigue without adding much extra stimulus, which is why a polarized distribution tends to outperform a threshold-heavy one over a season.

## Practical implications

Coaches should keep easy days genuinely easy, schedule hard sessions with enough recovery between them, and expand volume mainly through low-intensity work. Monitoring the intensity distribution over several weeks is more informative than judging single sessions.
